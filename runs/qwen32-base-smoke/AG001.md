# The Challenge of Fine-Tuning Models on Privacy-Sensitive Data

Fine-tuning large machine learning models on domain-specific data is a common and powerful practice that enables models to better adapt to specialized tasks and industries. However, this process often involves sensitive user data, raising significant privacy concerns. As models are trained on personal or proprietary data, there is a risk that the model might inadvertently memorize or leak private information, which can be exploited through attacks like membership inference or model inversion [citation:txt6]. This has led to a growing demand for privacy-preserving techniques that allow models to learn from data without compromising the confidentiality of individual users.

Differential privacy (DP) offers a rigorous and mathematically grounded framework for achieving this goal. By introducing calibrated noise into the training process, DP ensures that the output of a model remains statistically similar regardless of whether any one individual’s data is included or excluded [citation:txt11]. This guarantees a formal privacy bound, typically quantified by a privacy budget denoted as $\varepsilon$. In the context of machine learning, DP is often implemented through methods like DP-SGD (Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent), which involves gradient clipping and noise addition to maintain privacy while preserving model utility [citation:txt20].

Despite its promise, traditional DP approaches often operate at the example level, where each individual data point is protected. While this can offer some privacy guarantees, it falls short in scenarios where a single user contributes multiple examples to the training set. For instance, in federated learning or when fine-tuning large language models (LLMs), users may contribute extensive and correlated data. In such cases, example-level DP may not provide sufficient protection, as an attacker could still infer a user’s participation by analyzing the collective patterns in the model’s outputs [citation:txt7].

User-level differential privacy addresses this limitation by offering a stronger privacy guarantee. Instead of protecting individual examples, user-level DP ensures that the inclusion or exclusion of an entire user’s data has a negligible impact on the model. This is particularly important in modern data ecosystems, where users often contribute multiple, potentially sensitive examples, and where data ownership is increasingly centralized in datacenter settings [citation:txt8]. By treating each user’s data as a single unit, user-level DP prevents the model from being overly influenced by any one user’s contribution, thereby reducing the risk of privacy breaches [citation:txt5].

Implementing user-level DP in datacenter environments offers greater flexibility compared to federated learning, where communication and computational constraints often limit the scope of privacy-preserving techniques. This flexibility allows for the development of more sophisticated and scalable algorithms tailored for large-scale LLM fine-tuning. The central research question this article investigates is how to leverage this flexibility to design effective user-level DP algorithms that maintain strong privacy guarantees while minimizing the trade-offs in model performance and computational cost.

# Technical Foundations of User-Level Privacy in Model Training

Training models with user-level differential privacy (DP) requires a careful adaptation of standard optimization algorithms like stochastic gradient descent (SGD). At the core of this adaptation is DP-SGD, a variant of SGD that introduces privacy by clipping gradients to bound their influence and adding calibrated Gaussian noise to the gradient updates [citation:txt1]. This method ensures that the contribution of any individual example to the model’s training is limited and masked by noise, satisfying $( \epsilon, \delta )$-differential privacy [citation:txt2]. In the case of example-level DP, DP-SGD is sufficient to protect individual data points, but it falls short when the privacy unit is the user and their entire collection of examples must be protected [citation:txt6].

To extend DP-SGD to user-level DP, two primary strategies have emerged: Example-Level Sampling (ELS) and User-Level Sampling (ULS). In ELS, DP-SGD is applied directly to the dataset with increased noise to account for the multiple examples per user. This approach maintains the example-level gradient clipping but increases the noise scale to ensure that the cumulative effect of a user’s contributions is masked. However, this can lead to a significant degradation in model performance due to the higher noise levels [citation:txt3].

In contrast, ULS modifies the data sampling process by ensuring that each mini-batch contains at most one example per user. This limits the influence of any single user on the training process, thereby enabling user-level DP without requiring excessive noise. The preprocessing step in ULS involves bounding the number of examples each user can contribute, referred to as the 'contribution bound' [citation:txt3]. This bound is a critical hyperparameter that must be carefully optimized to balance privacy and model utility. A higher contribution bound may improve model performance by allowing more data to be used, but it also increases the risk of privacy violations if not properly constrained [citation:txt20].

The batch formation processes for ELS and ULS differ significantly. In ELS, mini-batches are formed by randomly sampling examples from the dataset, which can include multiple examples from the same user. Privacy is maintained through increased noise and gradient clipping, but this can result in higher variance and slower convergence [citation:txt3]. In ULS, mini-batches are constructed to ensure that no user contributes more than one example, which inherently limits the user’s influence and reduces the need for excessive noise. However, this approach can be more computationally intensive due to the additional constraints on batch formation [citation:txt3].

Both strategies require careful tuning of the contribution bound to achieve optimal results. In ELS, a lower bound may be necessary to maintain strong privacy guarantees, but this can lead to reduced model performance. In ULS, a higher bound may be acceptable if the diversity of user contributions is sufficient to maintain model utility without compromising privacy [citation:txt3]. The choice between ELS and ULS depends on the specific characteristics of the dataset and the desired privacy-utility trade-off.

These algorithmic adaptations demonstrate the feasibility of training large language models with user-level DP, but they also highlight the need for further optimizations to improve performance and scalability. The next section will explore these optimizations in more detail, focusing on how they can be leveraged to enhance the effectiveness of user-level DP in large-scale model fine-tuning.

# Algorithmic Optimizations for Effective User-Level DP in LLM Fine-Tuning

While user-level differential privacy (ULDP) offers a stronger privacy guarantee than example-level DP, its practical application to large language models (LLMs) has been hindered by the excessive noise traditionally required to maintain privacy. Recent theoretical advancements have enabled a more nuanced approach to privacy budgeting, significantly reducing the amount of noise needed without compromising the privacy guarantees. This is achieved through improved privacy accounting methods and tighter bounds on the sensitivity of gradients during training, allowing for a more efficient balance between model utility and privacy [citation:txt4]. These innovations are especially crucial for LLMs, where high-quality training data and large model sizes demand minimal performance degradation due to privacy-preserving mechanisms.

A key practical challenge in implementing ULDP is the selection of the contribution bound, which limits how much data from a single user can influence the training process. This parameter is critical in both Example-Level Sampling (ELS) and User-Level Sampling (ULS) approaches. In ELS, where each example is clipped individually, setting the contribution bound too high can lead to privacy violations, while setting it too low can limit model performance. To address this, researchers have proposed using the median number of examples per user as a principled and data-driven method for selecting the contribution bound. This approach avoids the costly trial-and-error process and ensures that the model can scale effectively to large datasets with heterogeneous user contributions [citation:txt5].

For ULS, the challenge is more complex, as the contribution bound applies to an entire user’s data. A novel predictive noise minimization approach has been introduced to optimize this bound by analyzing the expected impact of noise on model training under different privacy budgets. This method leverages the statistical properties of user data and training dynamics to predict the optimal contribution bound that minimizes the trade-off between privacy and model performance. By doing so, ULS can maintain strong privacy guarantees while allowing the model to learn effectively from each user’s diverse examples [citation:txt14]. 

These algorithmic optimizations are not just theoretical improvements but have tangible benefits in real-world LLM training. When applied to practical fine-tuning tasks, both ELS and ULS demonstrate improved performance, with ULS generally outperforming ELS in scenarios with large compute budgets or strong privacy requirements. The use of median-based contribution bounds and predictive noise minimization allows these algorithms to scale efficiently to models with hundreds of millions of parameters and datasets with hundreds of thousands of users [citation:txt15]. 

For example, in experiments with fixed compute budgets, ULS with optimized contribution bounds showed a marked reduction in loss compared to earlier approaches, particularly when users had a diverse set of examples [citation:txt7]. This is critical for natural language generation tasks, where user data is often varied and complex, and maintaining model quality is essential. The ability to train LLMs under these constraints without significant performance degradation makes user-level DP a viable option for organizations seeking to deploy privacy-preserving models in production environments.

In summary, the practical challenges of applying user-level DP to LLM fine-tuning have been addressed through algorithmic innovations that reduce noise, optimize contribution bounds, and improve training efficiency. These optimizations are foundational for enabling the widespread adoption of ULDP in real-world applications, ensuring that strong privacy guarantees do not come at the cost of model utility.

# Experimental Results on Optimized ELS and ULS for LLM Fine-Tuning

To evaluate the effectiveness of the optimized Example-Level Sampling (ELS) and User-Level Sampling (ULS) algorithms under user-level differential privacy (ULDP), we conducted extensive experiments on two standard datasets: StackOverflow and CC-News. These datasets were chosen for their relevance in natural language generation tasks and their realistic user-level contribution patterns. The results demonstrate that the proposed optimizations significantly reduce the amount of noise required to maintain privacy guarantees, while improving model performance over pre-trained baselines under strict privacy constraints.

A key theoretical contribution of this work is the derivation of new privacy bounds that show privacy guarantees decay near-linearly, rather than exponentially, with the contribution bound. This insight allows for more efficient noise calibration and reduces the overall privacy cost when bounding user contributions. As a result, the required noise magnitude is significantly reduced compared to prior methods, enabling better utility without compromising privacy[citation:txt11].

We fine-tuned a 350 million parameter transformer model using both ELS and ULS on the StackOverflow and CC-News datasets. In all settings, both methods outperformed the pre-trained model under the same privacy constraints, confirming the utility gains achievable through user-level DP fine-tuning. Notably, the optimized versions of ELS and ULS achieved these results while adhering to fixed compute budgets, demonstrating that privacy and performance can be balanced effectively[citation:txt5].

The results also highlight the superiority of ULS in most scenarios. Under high compute budgets and strong privacy requirements, ULS consistently outperformed ELS in terms of model utility. This is particularly evident in tasks where users contribute diverse and numerous examples, as ULS better captures the variability in user data through per-user gradient clipping and sampling. The performance gap between ULS and ELS is most pronounced in settings with large datasets and high privacy levels (ε ≤ 1), where ULS maintains significantly lower loss values[citation:txt16].

However, there are scenarios where ELS is more favorable. When privacy requirements are extremely high or compute resources are limited, ELS can be a more practical choice due to its lower computational overhead and simpler implementation. For instance, in low-budget settings, the performance of ELS and ULS converges, and ELS may even slightly outperform ULS due to its lower per-iteration cost[citation:txt15].

# Conclusion: Enabling Secure and Effective LLM Fine-Tuning with User-Level Differential Privacy

In conclusion, this work has demonstrated that user-level differential privacy (ULDP) can be effectively applied to large language model (LLM) fine-tuning, offering robust privacy guarantees while maintaining strong model performance. Through the development and optimization of Example-Level Sampling (ELS) and User-Level Sampling (ULS) algorithms, we have provided practical, well-justified parameter choices that make ULDP both feasible and efficient for real-world applications. These algorithms address the critical challenge of ensuring uniform privacy protection across users, especially in scenarios where users contribute multiple examples to the training set.

The optimized ELS and ULS algorithms introduced in this study significantly reduce the amount of noise required to maintain privacy, thereby minimizing the trade-off between privacy and utility. This is achieved through novel privacy accounting techniques and heuristic parameter selection strategies, such as using the median user example count for ELS and a predictive noise minimization approach for ULS. These optimizations are essential for scaling ULDP to LLMs with hundreds of millions of parameters and datasets with hundreds of thousands of users [citation:txt4].

Importantly, the experimental results confirm that fine-tuning LLMs with user-level DP is not only feasible but also advantageous over relying solely on pre-trained models. Under strict privacy constraints, the fine-tuned models using ULDP consistently outperformed pre-trained baselines in terms of downstream task performance. This indicates that user-level DP does not merely act as a constraint but can also enhance model quality by encouraging better generalization and reducing overfitting to sensitive user data [citation:txt7].

The practical implications of these findings are significant. They enable secure model training on sensitive datasets—such as those found in healthcare, finance, and personal communication—without compromising the utility of the resulting models. This is particularly important in industries where data privacy regulations are stringent, and the risk of model memorization and data leakage is high. By providing a scalable and effective approach to user-level DP, this work paves the way for the deployment of LLMs in privacy-sensitive environments with confidence in both model performance and user protection [citation:txt10].

Ultimately, the contributions of this work extend beyond the technical innovations in algorithm design. They represent a step forward in the broader effort to align AI development with ethical and legal standards for data privacy. As LLMs continue to grow in size and capability, the ability to fine-tune them with strong privacy guarantees will become increasingly essential. The methods and insights presented here provide a solid foundation for future research and development in this critical area [citation:txt14].
