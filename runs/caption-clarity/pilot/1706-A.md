# The Challenge of Privacy in Fine-Tuning Large Language Models

Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) on domain-specific data is often essential for achieving high performance in specialized applications such as healthcare, finance, or enterprise settings. However, such data is frequently privacy-sensitive, containing personal or confidential information that must be protected. This creates a critical challenge: how can we adapt LLMs to domain-specific needs without compromising the privacy of the individuals whose data is used in the process?

Differential privacy (DP) has emerged as a powerful framework for addressing this challenge. By introducing carefully calibrated noise into the training process, DP ensures that the output of a model remains insensitive to the presence or absence of any single data point [citation:txt1]. This provides a formal mathematical guarantee of privacy, which is particularly important when training on data that might be linked to individuals.

However, when applied at the *example-level*, DP has limitations. Example-level DP ensures that the removal of a single data point does not significantly affect the model’s output. While this is effective for protecting individual examples, it falls short when a single user contributes multiple examples. In such cases, an attacker could potentially infer a user’s participation by analyzing patterns across multiple data points [citation:txt6]. This is especially concerning in scenarios where users contribute many examples, such as in federated learning or in the training of LLMs using user-generated text [citation:txt8].

To address these limitations, *user-level differential privacy* has been introduced. User-level DP ensures that the inclusion or exclusion of a user’s *entire* contribution—regardless of how many examples they provide—has a negligible impact on the model’s output [citation:txt5]. This provides a much stronger privacy guarantee, particularly in settings where users may contribute large amounts of correlated data, such as in language modeling tasks. For example, a user’s entire collection of emails or social media posts could be included in the training set, and user-level DP ensures that this data cannot be used to infer the user’s identity or specific content [citation:txt7].

Implementing user-level DP is particularly relevant in *datacenter training* environments, where training is centralized and more flexible compared to federated learning. In federated learning, user-level DP is naturally supported because each user’s data is aggregated as a single update. In contrast, datacenter settings require explicit design choices to ensure that user-level privacy is maintained. However, this increased flexibility also opens up new opportunities for algorithmic optimizations that can improve both privacy and model performance [citation:txt9].

This article investigates how user-level DP can be scaled to the training of large language models in datacenter settings. We explore the algorithmic techniques that enable user-level DP, such as user-level sampling and gradient clipping, and examine the trade-offs between privacy, model utility, and computational efficiency. The central research question is: how can we leverage the flexibility of datacenter training to implement user-level DP in a way that is both effective and scalable for LLMs?
