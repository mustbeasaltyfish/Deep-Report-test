# Introduction: Learning with Not Enough Data

In many real-world machine learning applications, the availability of labeled data is severely limited due to the high cost, time, or expertise required for annotation. Supervised learning, which traditionally relies on large volumes of labeled examples, struggles to perform well under such constraints. To address this challenge, researchers have developed a variety of strategies that either reduce the reliance on labeled data or augment it with additional information from unlabeled samples. This article is part of a series titled *Learning with Not Enough Data*, and it specifically focuses on **semi-supervised learning**, a powerful paradigm that leverages both labeled and unlabeled data to improve model performance. Before diving into the nuances of semi-supervised learning, it is important to understand the broader set of approaches that are commonly used when labeled data is scarce.

The four main strategies for learning with limited labeled data are as follows:

1. **Pre-training + Fine-tuning**: This approach involves training a model on a large, unlabeled dataset using unsupervised or self-supervised learning, followed by fine-tuning on a small labeled dataset. This method has been particularly effective in domains like natural language processing (NLP), where pre-trained models such as BERT or Llama can be adapted to downstream tasks with minimal labeled examples [citation:txt16].

2. **Semi-supervised Learning**: This method explicitly incorporates unlabeled data into the training process by making assumptions about the data distribution, such as the cluster or smoothness assumption. It typically involves techniques like pseudo labeling, consistency regularization, and graph-based methods to guide the learning of a model [citation:txt1].

3. **Active Learning**: Active learning reduces the need for large labeled datasets by allowing the model to interactively select the most informative samples for annotation. By prioritizing uncertain or high-value data points, this approach can significantly reduce annotation costs while maintaining or even improving model accuracy [citation:txt19].

4. **Pre-training + Dataset Auto-generation**: In this strategy, a pre-trained model is used to generate additional labeled data, which can then be used to train or fine-tune another model. This has been especially popular in NLP, where few-shot learning and large language models are leveraged to create synthetic datasets that mirror the target task [citation:txt7].

Each of these approaches offers a unique way to mitigate the limitations of small labeled datasets, and their effectiveness often depends on the specific characteristics of the data and the learning task. While all four are valuable, this article will focus on the **semi-supervised learning** approach, which has gained significant traction in recent years due to its ability to scale effectively with unlabeled data and its compatibility with deep learning architectures [citation:txt5].

Semi-supervised learning is conceptually situated between fully supervised and unsupervised learning, and it has evolved alongside the broader trends in machine learning, particularly the rise of neural networks and generative models [citation:txt5]. In the following sections, we will explore the theoretical foundations of semi-supervised learning, examine recent advances in techniques like pseudo labeling and consistency regularization, and discuss how pre-training can be integrated with semi-supervised methods to achieve state-of-the-art results in low-data regimes.
