# Introduction to the Pew Research Center Survey on Astrology, Tarot Cards, and Fortune-Telling

In a world increasingly shaped by science and technology, many Americans continue to turn to age-old practices such as astrology, tarot cards, and fortune-telling for guidance and entertainment. To better understand the prevalence and cultural significance of these practices, the Pew Research Center conducted a nationally representative survey of 9,593 U.S. adults in October 2024. The survey, part of the American Trends Panel, aimed to assess how often these practices are used, which demographic groups are most engaged, and whether they are viewed as a form of fun or as a tool for major life decisions [citation:txt1].

The survey results reveal that 30% of U.S. adults consult astrology, tarot cards, or a fortune teller at least once a year [citation:txt3]. While this number may seem surprising in a scientifically literate society, it reflects a broader cultural shift toward New Age and spiritual practices, particularly among younger generations. This trend has been amplified by the rise of social media, where astrology and tarot content often goes viral and attracts millions of followers. The pandemic also played a role in this resurgence, as many individuals turned to these practices during periods of uncertainty and isolation [citation:txt14].

The survey was carefully weighted to ensure that the sample accurately represented the U.S. adult population across key demographic factors such as age, gender, race, and income. This methodological rigor allows for reliable insights into the patterns of belief and engagement with these practices. As the following sections will explore in greater depth, the data reveals significant differences in how various groups interact with astrology, tarot cards, and fortune-telling, offering a nuanced picture of their role in contemporary American life.

# Prevalence and Frequency of Engagement with Astrology, Tarot Cards, and Fortune-Telling

According to the Pew Research Center’s 2024 survey, 30% of U.S. adults consult astrology, tarot cards, or fortune tellers at least once a year [citation:txt6]. This figure includes those who engage in one or more of these practices, with astrology being the most common. Specifically, 28% of Americans consult astrology or horoscopes at least once or twice a year, a figure that closely mirrors the 27% who say they believe in astrology [citation:txt11]. In contrast, about one in ten Americans consult tarot cards annually, and only 6% consult a fortune teller at least once a year [citation:img1].

Despite the relatively high rate of engagement, most Americans use these practices for entertainment rather than as a source of serious life guidance. Just 10% of those who consult astrology, tarot cards, or fortune tellers do so because they believe the practices offer meaningful insights into their lives, while 20% say they engage in these activities primarily for fun [citation:txt10]. This trend is especially pronounced among younger adults, with 23% of those aged 18 to 29 saying they consult these practices for entertainment, compared to only 6% of those aged 50 to 64 [citation:txt18].

Belief in astrology, however, has remained largely stable over the past seven years. In 2024, 27% of U.S. adults said they believe in astrology, a slight decrease from 29% in 2017 [citation:img2]. This stability suggests that astrology has become a relatively mainstream part of American culture, rather than a growing or declining trend. Religious affiliation influences these beliefs, with religiously unaffiliated individuals being more likely to believe in astrology than their religiously affiliated counterparts [citation:img3].

Demographic differences are particularly pronounced when it comes to engagement with astrology and tarot cards. Younger adults, especially younger women, are far more likely to consult and believe in these practices. For example, 43% of women aged 18 to 49 say they believe in astrology, compared to 27% of women aged 50 and older, 20% of men aged 18 to 49, and 15% of men aged 50 and older [citation:txt7]. This gender gap becomes more pronounced with age, as older women are more likely than older men to believe in astrology [citation:txt3]. Additionally, the LGBTQ+ community shows a higher level of engagement with astrology, with 54% of LGBTQ+ adults consulting horoscopes annually and 33% engaging with tarot cards—three times the national average [citation:txt13].

These findings highlight a broader generational and gendered shift in how Americans approach spiritual and esoteric practices. While astrology, tarot cards, and fortune-telling are not central to most people’s decision-making processes, they remain a popular and culturally accepted form of entertainment and self-reflection, particularly among younger and more secular populations.

# Demographic Variations in Belief and Engagement with Astrology, Tarot Cards, and Fortune-Telling

Belief in and engagement with astrology, tarot cards, and fortune-telling practices in the United States are not evenly distributed across demographic groups. These practices show distinct patterns based on factors such as gender, age, religious affiliation, sexual orientation, income, political ideology, education, and race/ethnicity. The Pew Research Center survey of 9,593 U.S. adults, conducted in October 2024, reveals a complex landscape of belief and participation that reflects broader cultural and social trends.

Gender is one of the most significant predictors of engagement with these practices. Women are about twice as likely as men to believe in astrology, with 35% of women expressing belief compared to 18% of men [citation:txt9]. This disparity is especially pronounced among younger women: 43% of women aged 18 to 49 believe in astrology, compared to 20% of men in the same age group [citation:txt2]. The gender gap in belief also shifts with age, with older women being significantly more likely than older men to express belief. For example, women between the ages of 45 and 64 are twice as likely as their male counterparts to say they believe in astrology (29% vs. 15%), and women aged 65 and older are more than twice as likely as older men to believe (23% vs. 9%) [citation:txt1].

LGBT adults are also more likely to engage with these practices than their non-LGBT counterparts. In fact, 43% of LGBT adults believe in astrology, compared to 26% of non-LGBT adults [citation:txt9]. Furthermore, 54% of LGBT adults consult astrology or horoscopes at least once a year, a rate roughly twice that of the general U.S. adult population [citation:txt4]. These trends underscore the role of identity and community in shaping spiritual and esoteric practices, particularly among groups that may feel a stronger need for alternative sources of meaning and connection.

Religious affiliation also plays a role in belief in and engagement with astrology. While religiously affiliated and unaffiliated Americans are equally likely to believe in astrology (27% and 28%, respectively), significant differences exist within specific religious groups. For instance, about one-third of Black Protestants, Hispanic Catholics, and individuals who say their religion is “nothing in particular” believe in astrology [citation:txt6]. In contrast, atheists, White evangelical Protestants, and Jewish Americans are less likely than the general public to embrace these beliefs [citation:txt6].

Socioeconomic and political factors also influence engagement with astrology. Lower-income adults are about twice as likely as those in the upper-income category to believe in astrology (37% vs. 16%) [citation:txt9]. Similarly, politically liberal individuals are more likely to express belief in astrology than conservatives [citation:txt9]. Education level also plays a role, with adults without college degrees more likely than college graduates to believe in astrology [citation:txt9].

Race and ethnicity further shape patterns of belief. Black Americans are more likely than White and Asian Americans to believe in astrology [citation:txt9]. This trend aligns with broader data showing that Black and Hispanic Americans are more likely than White Americans to engage in a range of New Age beliefs, including psychics, spiritual energy, and reincarnation [citation:img6].

# Demographic Patterns and Frequency of Engagement with Astrology, Tarot Cards, and Fortune-Telling

The Pew Research Center survey reveals distinct patterns in how often Americans engage with astrology, tarot cards, and fortune-telling, with significant variation across demographic groups. Overall, 28% of U.S. adults consult astrology or horoscopes at least once or twice a year, with the practice being particularly common among younger adults, women, and members of religiously unaffiliated groups. Notably, Hispanic Catholics, Black Protestants, and those who identify with "nothing in particular" as their religious affiliation are among the most likely to consult astrology or horoscopes annually [citation:img1].

Tarot card consultation is less common than astrology, with about 11% of U.S. adults reporting they consult tarot cards at least once a year. However, this number rises sharply among specific subgroups, particularly the LGBTQ+ community. A striking 33% of LGBT adults consult tarot cards annually, which is three times the national average. This pattern is even more pronounced when looking at frequency: 15% of LGBT adults consult tarot cards at least once or twice a month [citation:txt1]. These findings highlight a strong connection between New Age spiritual practices and the LGBTQ+ community, a trend that has been noted in both academic and media discussions [citation:txt7].

Fortune-telling is the least common of the three practices, with only 6% of U.S. adults consulting fortune tellers at least once or twice a year. However, this behavior is more prevalent among certain age and racial/ethnic groups. For example, 14% of adults ages 18 to 29 consult fortune tellers annually, compared to just 2% of those 65 and older. Additionally, Asian Americans are more likely to consult fortune tellers than the general population, with 15% reporting annual consultations [citation:img1]. These variations suggest that cultural and generational factors play a role in shaping engagement with fortune-telling.

Religious affiliation also strongly influences whether individuals engage in these practices. White evangelicals and atheists are among the least likely to consult astrology, tarot cards, or fortune tellers. In fact, 83% of white evangelicals say they never consult any of these practices, making them the least engaged group in the survey [citation:img3]. Conversely, religiously unaffiliated individuals, especially those who identify as agnostic or as having "nothing in particular" as their religion, are more likely to consult astrology or tarot cards than their religiously affiliated counterparts [citation:txt9].

These demographic differences underscore the complex relationship between spirituality, identity, and cultural trends in contemporary American society. While some groups embrace these practices as part of their personal or spiritual exploration, others view them with skepticism or disinterest. The next section will examine how Americans use these practices in their lives, including whether they rely on them for fun or for making major life decisions.

# Motivations for Engaging in Astrology, Tarot Cards, and Fortune-Telling

The motivations behind Americans' engagement with astrology, tarot cards, and fortune-telling reveal a nuanced picture of how these practices are perceived and used across different demographics. According to the Pew Research Center survey, 20% of U.S. adults engage in these practices mostly for fun, while 10% do so primarily because they believe the practices offer helpful insights [citation:txt4]. This distinction is critical in understanding the role these practices play in everyday life, as it separates recreational interest from a more serious reliance on them for guidance.

LGBT adults stand out as the demographic most likely to seek helpful insights from these practices. Specifically, 29% of LGBT adults engage with astrology, tarot cards, or fortune-telling for insights, compared to the national average of 10% [citation:txt6]. This is reflected in their reliance on these practices for major life decisions, with 21% of LGBT adults reporting that they rely at least a little on such insights when making significant choices [citation:txt12]. This is more than four times the national average of 5% [citation:txt5].

Black Americans and younger Americans also show a higher likelihood of engaging with these practices for insights. Nearly one in five Black Americans (19%) and Americans under the age of 30 (17%) consult astrology, tarot cards, or fortune-telling for helpful guidance [citation:txt6]. This is particularly notable among young women, with 17% of those aged 18 to 29 reporting that they consult these practices for insights [citation:img6].

Despite these trends, only a small minority of Americans rely heavily on these practices for making major life decisions. Overall, 1% of U.S. adults rely a lot on insights from astrology, tarot cards, or fortune-telling, while an additional 5% rely at least a little [citation:txt5]. However, certain groups deviate from this pattern. Hispanic Catholics, for instance, are more likely to rely on these practices for decision-making, with 12% reporting at least a little reliance [citation:img1]. Similarly, 21% of LGBT adults report relying at least a little on these practices for major life decisions, a rate significantly higher than the general population [citation:txt10].

These findings highlight how cultural and social identities shape the use of New Age practices in the U.S. While the majority of Americans engage in these practices for entertainment or curiosity, specific demographic groups—particularly those who identify as LGBT, Hispanic Catholic, Black, or under 30—tend to view these practices as more than just a form of leisure. Instead, they are integrated into their decision-making processes, reflecting the diverse ways in which Americans navigate spirituality, identity, and personal guidance in an increasingly complex world.
