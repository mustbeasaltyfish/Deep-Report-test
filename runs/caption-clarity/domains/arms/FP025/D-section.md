# Context and Methodology of Pandemic-Related Mobility

The onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in early 2020 triggered a significant shift in American mobility patterns. In the first few months of 2020, slightly more than 3 percent of the adult American population left their residence and went elsewhere due to the outbreak of the virus, with another 8.5 percent moving for reasons not associated with the pandemic. This resulted in a total of 11.6 percent of respondents to Wave 68 of the Pew Research Center’s American Trends Panel (ATP) reporting a residential move, either due to the pandemic or other causes. This migration rate was notably higher than the 9 percent annual migration rate reported by the U.S. Census Bureau for the same year[citation:txt1].

The initial wave of moves was primarily driven by health concerns and the sudden closures of college campuses. Young adults, in particular, were among the most affected demographic, with many relocating to their family homes or other safer locations to avoid exposure to the virus[citation:txt19]. As the pandemic progressed, the motivations for moving evolved, incorporating financial and economic factors alongside health-related concerns.

To better understand these changes, the Pew Research Center conducted two major surveys in 2020: one in June and another in November. These surveys tracked the shifting motivations and destinations of Americans who relocated during the pandemic. The data was drawn from the ATP, an online survey panel recruited through national, random sampling of residential addresses, ensuring that nearly all U.S. adults had a chance of selection. The sample was weighted to be representative of the U.S. adult population by gender, race, ethnicity, partisan affiliation, education, and other demographic categories[citation:txt12].

The surveys included a total of 12,648 U.S. adults, and the results from the June and November surveys were compared to examine the evolving dynamics of pandemic-induced migration. The ATP methodology ensures that the data is nationally representative and provides a robust foundation for analyzing migration trends during the pandemic[citation:txt3].

In addition to demographic considerations, the Pew Research Center also defined income tiers for the purposes of analysis. The terms “middle income,” “lower income,” and “upper income” were based on adjusted median family income, allowing for a nuanced understanding of how financial status influenced migration decisions during the pandemic[citation:txt1].

It is important to note that the Asian American sample in these surveys included only English speakers and may not fully represent the broader Asian American population. This limitation is significant, as the Asian American community is diverse and includes many individuals who may not have been captured in the English-only survey[citation:txt18]. Despite this, the findings from the survey are valuable in understanding the broader context of pandemic-related mobility across different demographic groups.

By analyzing these early migration patterns and survey data, researchers can begin to draw insights into how the pandemic reshaped American mobility and what factors most strongly influenced relocation decisions during this unprecedented period.
