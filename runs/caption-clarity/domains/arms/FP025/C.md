# The Early Pandemic Migration Surge and the Pew Research Center Surveys

The outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in early 2020 triggered a significant shift in Americans' mobility patterns. In the first few months of 2020, slightly more than 3 percent of the adult population in the United States relocated due to the pandemic, while an additional 8.5 percent moved for reasons unrelated to the crisis, resulting in an overall migration rate of 11.6 percent among respondents in Wave 68 of the Pew Research Center’s American Trends Panel (ATP) [citation:txt1]. This was notably higher than the annual migration rate of slightly less than 9 percent reported by the Current Population Survey (CPS) for the same year. While the discrepancy between these figures is partly attributed to the smaller sample size of the Pew survey compared to the CPS, it also reflects the dynamic and often temporary nature of many pandemic-related relocations, with some individuals returning to their original residences by year’s end [citation:txt1].

The initial wave of pandemic-induced moves was largely driven by health concerns and the abrupt closure of college campuses. Many young adults, particularly students, were forced to leave dormitories and move back in with family members, while others relocated to safer or more isolated areas to avoid the spread of the virus. These early relocations were often temporary, but they laid the groundwork for more complex and evolving migration patterns as the pandemic progressed [citation:txt19].

To better understand how these patterns evolved, the Pew Research Center conducted two major surveys in June and November 2020. These surveys tracked changes in moving behavior, motivations, and destinations over time. The data was collected from a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults through the ATP, a longitudinal survey panel recruited via a random sampling of residential addresses. This approach ensured that nearly all U.S. adults had a chance of selection, and the sample was weighted to reflect the U.S. adult population by key demographic factors such as gender, race, ethnicity, education, and income [citation:txt12].

The survey also included a detailed breakdown of income tiers, which were defined based on adjusted median family income. The Pew Research Center categorized respondents as “lower income,” “middle income,” and “upper income” to analyze how different economic groups were affected by the pandemic’s mobility shift. These definitions were essential for understanding the intersection of financial stability and relocation decisions during a period of widespread economic uncertainty [citation:txt19].

It is important to note, however, that while the Pew Research Center strives for comprehensive representation, certain limitations exist in specific demographic samples. For example, the Asian American sample in the surveys included only English speakers, which may not fully reflect the broader and linguistically diverse Asian American population [citation:txt18]. Despite this limitation, the survey still provides valuable insights into the pandemic’s impact on mobility patterns among a wide range of demographic groups.

By examining these early pandemic migration motivations and the methodological rigor of the Pew Research Center’s surveys, we can begin to understand the broader implications of the pandemic on American mobility. The next section will explore the evolving reasons for relocation as the pandemic continued and how these motivations shifted over time.

# Prevalence and Demographic Breakdown of Pandemic-Related Moves

As the pandemic progressed, the prevalence of pandemic-related moves among Americans increased, with distinct demographic patterns emerging between June and November 2020. According to the Pew Research Center’s surveys, 3% of U.S. adults reported moving due to the pandemic in June 2020, but this figure rose to 5% by November 2020. In total, 10% of adults in November said they had either moved themselves or had someone move in with them due to the outbreak, marking a slight increase from 8% in June [citation:txt19].

This shift in movement rates highlights the evolving nature of pandemic-related relocations, which were initially driven by immediate health concerns and later influenced by broader social and economic factors. Notably, young adults (ages 18–29) were the most likely demographic group to move due to the pandemic, with 11% reporting such a move in November. This age group’s high mobility can be attributed to factors such as college closures, remote work opportunities, and the desire to live with family or partners [citation:txt19].

In addition to age, income and race/ethnicity also played a significant role in determining who was more likely to move during the pandemic. Lower-income adults (9%) were more likely to move than middle- or upper-income adults (4%), reflecting the economic pressures and instability faced by those with limited financial resources. Similarly, Hispanic (9%) and Black (7%) adults were more likely to relocate than White adults (4%), indicating disparities in how different racial and ethnic groups navigated the challenges of the pandemic [citation:txt19].

These findings are supported by broader data on migration patterns during the early months of the pandemic. According to the National Association of Realtors, 8.95 million people moved residences between March and October 2020, slightly higher than the 8.82 million who moved during the same period in 2019. The increase in migration during the early months of the pandemic, particularly in March and April, was likely driven by pandemic-related considerations rather than pre-existing migration trends [citation:txt1].

To further illustrate the demographic breakdown of pandemic-related moves, consider the following data from the Pew Research Center. In November 2020, young adults (18–29) were the most likely to have moved due to the pandemic, at 11%, while those aged 65 and older had the lowest rate of movement at 2%. In terms of race and ethnicity, Hispanic adults had the highest rate of pandemic-related moves at 9%, followed by Asian adults at 6% and Black adults at 7%. These disparities underscore the uneven impact of the pandemic on different demographic groups, particularly in terms of access to resources and the ability to relocate [citation:txt7].

# Evolving Reasons for Pandemic-Related Moves: A Shift Toward Financial Pressures

As the pandemic progressed, the motivations behind Americans’ decisions to move shifted significantly, reflecting the evolving challenges brought on by the crisis. In June 2020, the primary reasons for pandemic-related relocations were largely health- and education-related. For instance, 28% of respondents cited a desire to reduce their risk of contracting the virus, while 23% moved due to the closure of their college campuses or the shift to online learning [citation:txt11]. However, by November 2020, financial concerns had become the dominant reason, with 32% of movers citing job loss or other financial problems—up from 18% in June [citation:txt1]. This marked a notable transition from initial pandemic fears to the long-term economic consequences of the crisis.

The increasing emphasis on financial pressures was not the only change; the relative importance of other motivations also shifted. For example, the share of Americans who moved to be closer to family or a partner decreased from 20% in June to 17% in November [citation:txt1]. Similarly, the percentage of those who moved due to the perceived risk of living in areas with high infection rates dropped from 28% to 14%. College closures and local restrictions also saw declines, from 23% to 14% and from 12%, respectively [citation:txt1]. These changes suggest that as the pandemic continued, the immediate health-related anxieties that initially drove relocations gave way to more practical concerns, particularly around employment and financial stability.

Financial pressures not only influenced those who moved but also affected those who had someone move in with them. By November 2020, 36% of individuals who had a new housemate cited financial problems as the main reason for the move [citation:txt1]. This underscores how economic hardship was reshaping household structures and living arrangements across the country. The growing reliance on shared housing as a financial coping strategy was a clear indicator of the broader economic strain many Americans faced during the pandemic.

The data also reveals that while financial concerns became more prevalent, they did not completely overshadow other motivations. The desire to be near family or a partner remained a significant reason, suggesting that social and emotional needs continued to play a role in relocation decisions. Additionally, a small but notable portion of Americans still moved to avoid areas with high infection rates or to escape strict local restrictions. However, these reasons became less dominant as time passed, reinforcing the idea that the pandemic's economic fallout was increasingly shaping mobility patterns.

As shown, financial reasons saw the most substantial increase, rising from 18% to 33%, while health-related and educational concerns declined sharply [citation:img1]. # Changes in Destinations and Living Arrangements of Pandemic Movers

As the pandemic progressed, the destinations and living arrangements of those who relocated due to the crisis underwent notable shifts. In June 2020, a significant majority of pandemic-related movers—61%—chose to move in with family members, likely driven by a combination of health concerns, the closure of colleges, and early economic uncertainty [citation:txt10]. However, by November 2020, this share had dropped to 42%, indicating a change in priorities or circumstances among Americans [citation:txt1]. This decline may reflect the increasing normalization of the pandemic, a reduced sense of urgency to seek shelter with relatives, or a shift in economic conditions that made independent housing more feasible or necessary.

Conversely, the percentage of movers who rented or purchased a new home increased from 9% in June to 16% in November [citation:txt1]. This trend could be attributed to the growing feasibility of remote work, which reduced the need to live near traditional urban centers, and the availability of government assistance programs that provided temporary financial relief and helped stabilize housing markets [citation:txt2]. In addition, 10% of pandemic movers relocated to a vacation home by November, and 8% moved in with a friend, suggesting a diverse set of motivations and living preferences [citation:txt7].

The shift in living arrangements also reveals patterns in who moved in with whom. Among those who hosted someone due to the pandemic, 51% reported that it was an adult child or child’s spouse, while 23% said it was a parent or parent-in-law. Other common arrangements included friends (29%) and romantic partners (24%), with the remaining cases involving other relationships [citation:txt12]. These patterns highlight the central role of family in pandemic-era housing decisions and the broader societal trend of young adults returning to live with their parents. In fact, by July 2020, a majority of young adults were living with their parents, a phenomenon not seen since the Great Depression [citation:txt10].

The evolving nature of these living arrangements also reflects broader economic and social shifts. As the pandemic continued, financial pressures became a more prominent factor in relocation decisions, with fewer people citing the immediate health risks of the virus as their primary motivation [citation:txt2]. This financial shift likely influenced the decline in family-based relocations and the rise in independent housing solutions, as individuals sought to balance cost, safety, and long-term stability [citation:txt2].

These changes in destinations and living arrangements during the pandemic also had lasting implications. For example, about 58% of those who had people move in with them due to the pandemic reported that at least one of them was still there by the end of 2020, suggesting that some pandemic-era living arrangements had transitioned from temporary to more permanent solutions [citation:txt1]. At the same time, 39% of people who had someone move in with them no longer had anyone living with them due to the pandemic, indicating that some relocations were indeed temporary [citation:txt1].

The visual representation of these shifts further illustrates the changing landscape of pandemic-related relocations. A bar chart comparing June and November 2020 data shows a clear decline in the percentage of people moving in with family and a corresponding increase in independent housing options [citation:img1]. This trend underscores the dynamic nature of the pandemic’s impact on American living arrangements and highlights the resilience and adaptability of individuals and families during a period of unprecedented uncertainty.

# The Permanence of Pandemic-Related Moves and Living Arrangements

As the pandemic continued to shape the American landscape, the permanence of pandemic-related relocations became a key area of interest. According to the Pew Research Center’s November 2020 survey, 69% of individuals who had moved due to the pandemic were still living in a different home at that time. This group was composed of 40% who had settled in a new community and 29% who had found a new home within their original community. In contrast, 31% of pandemic movers had returned to their pre-pandemic residences by November 2020, indicating a significant but not overwhelming trend of returning to prior living situations [citation:txt3].

The decision to remain in a new location or return was influenced by a variety of factors, including the reasons for the initial move, financial stability, and personal circumstances. Those who relocated due to financial pressures or health-related concerns were more likely to remain in their new homes, as these motivations often reflected deeper, longer-term shifts in lifestyle and economic conditions [citation:txt11].

In addition to examining where pandemic movers ended up, researchers also looked at the lasting impact of shared living arrangements. The Pew Research Center found that 58% of individuals who had taken in someone during the pandemic still had at least one person living with them as of November 2020. Meanwhile, 39% had no additional housemates remaining. These figures underscore the complex and often temporary nature of pandemic-induced household changes, as many people moved in together out of necessity but later moved out as circumstances improved [citation:txt3].

The persistence of new living arrangements, both in terms of relocation and shared housing, reflected broader societal shifts. For example, the increase in remote work and the decline in urban density due to the pandemic led to a reevaluation of where and how people chose to live. Some individuals moved to more affordable areas or into shared housing as a way to cope with economic uncertainty, while others sought out new communities that offered better access to resources or a better quality of life [citation:txt17].

These trends were not uniform across all demographic groups. Younger adults, for instance, were more likely to move in with family or friends during the pandemic and often remained in those arrangements for extended periods. Similarly, individuals with lower incomes were more likely to experience temporary or permanent housing changes due to financial instability [citation:txt13].

The data from the Pew Research Center surveys highlights the fact that many pandemic-related moves were not temporary disruptions but rather significant, long-term shifts in the American housing landscape. As the pandemic’s impact continues to unfold, the question of how many of these changes will persist remains an open one, with implications for urban development, housing policy, and the future of American mobility.

# Perceived Outcomes of Pandemic-Related Moves Versus Other Moves

The relocation decisions made by Americans during the pandemic were often shaped by urgent and uncertain circumstances, and these differences are reflected in the perceptions of the move’s impact. According to the Pew Research Center’s November 2020 survey, only 43% of individuals who moved due to the pandemic reported that their relocation had a positive impact on their lives, compared to 61% of those who moved for other reasons. In contrast, 29% of pandemic movers described the impact of their move as negative, compared to just 12% of those who moved for other reasons. These findings underscore the complex and often challenging nature of pandemic-related relocations, which were frequently driven by factors such as health concerns, job loss, or the need to live closer to family rather than by personal choice or long-term planning [citation:txt19].

One possible explanation for the lower rates of positive perception among pandemic movers is the abrupt and often involuntary nature of their relocations. Many individuals were forced to move due to financial hardship, health risks, or changes in work and education environments, which may have made the transition more stressful and less satisfying. In contrast, those who moved for other reasons—such as career opportunities, personal preferences, or lifestyle changes—may have had greater control over their decisions and were more likely to view the move as a positive step forward [citation:txt10].

The data also reveals that a larger proportion of pandemic movers (28%) described the impact of their move as “neither positive nor negative,” compared to 25% of other movers. This suggests that for a significant share of pandemic-related relocations, the experience may have been neutral or mixed, without a clear positive or negative outcome. This could reflect the uncertainty and instability of the pandemic period, during which many people were still adjusting to their new living situations and assessing the long-term implications of their moves [citation:txt19].

The differences in perceived outcomes between the two groups also highlight the broader socioeconomic and psychological toll of the pandemic. As noted in previous sections, financial pressures became a more prominent factor in relocation decisions over time, and many pandemic movers faced significant economic challenges that may have limited their ability to achieve a positive outcome from their move [citation:txt6]. Additionally, the stress and uncertainty associated with the pandemic likely contributed to higher rates of dissatisfaction and negative perceptions among those who relocated during this period [citation:txt9].
