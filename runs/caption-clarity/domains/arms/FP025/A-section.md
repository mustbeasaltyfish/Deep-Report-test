# Context and Methodology of Pandemic-Related Mobility

The onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in early 2020 triggered a significant shift in American mobility patterns. In the first few months of 2020, slightly more than 3 percent of the adult American population left their residence and went elsewhere due to the outbreak of the virus, with another 8.5 percent moving for reasons not associated with the pandemic. This resulted in a total of 11.6 percent of respondents to Wave 68 of the Pew Research Center’s American Trends Panel (ATP) reporting a residential move, either due to the pandemic or other causes. This migration rate was notably higher than the 2020 annual migration rate reported by the Current Population Survey (CPS), which recorded slightly less than 9 percent [citation:txt1]. However, the discrepancy between these two data sets is partly attributed to the fact that the Pew sample is much smaller than the CPS, making them not strictly comparable. Additionally, some coronavirus-related migrants may not have settled permanently in their new locations by the end of 2020, and thus would not have been captured in the CPS, which simply asks whether respondents are in a different house than 12 months before [citation:txt1].

The early wave of pandemic-related moves was largely driven by health concerns and the closure of college campuses. Many young adults, particularly those who were students, were forced to leave dormitories and return to their family homes. Others moved to be closer to family for emotional support or to reduce the risk of infection. These initial relocations were often temporary but had lasting implications on housing demand and residential distribution across the country [citation:txt19].

To better understand these shifting patterns, the Pew Research Center conducted two major surveys in 2020: one in June and another in November. These surveys aimed to capture the evolving motivations, destinations, and living arrangements of Americans who moved during the pandemic. The data was collected from a nationally representative sample via the American Trends Panel, an online survey panel recruited through national, random sampling of residential addresses. This method ensures that nearly all U.S. adults have an equal chance of being selected, and the sample is weighted to be representative of the U.S. adult population by gender, race, ethnicity, partisan affiliation, education, and other demographic categories [citation:txt12].

In defining income tiers for the study, the Pew Research Center used adjusted median family income as a benchmark. The categories of 'lower income,' 'middle income,' and 'upper income' were established based on this metric, allowing for a nuanced analysis of how different economic groups were affected by pandemic-related relocations [citation:txt1]. Special attention was also given to the representation of Asian Americans in the survey. While the Pew Research Center made efforts to include a representative sample of Asian American adults, the survey was conducted in English and may not fully represent the broader Asian American population, particularly those who are not fluent in English [citation:txt18].

By examining the data collected in these two waves of the ATP, the Pew Research Center sought to not only document the immediate effects of the pandemic on mobility but also to understand how these patterns evolved over time. This foundational analysis sets the stage for a deeper exploration of the motivations, destinations, and demographic implications of pandemic-era relocations in the following sections.
