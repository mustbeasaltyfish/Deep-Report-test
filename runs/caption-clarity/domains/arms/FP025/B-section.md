# Context and Methodology of Pandemic-Related Mobility

The onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in early 2020 triggered a significant shift in American mobility patterns. In the first few months of 2020, slightly more than 3 percent of the adult American population left their residence and went elsewhere due to the outbreak of the virus, with another 8.5 percent moving for reasons not associated with the pandemic. This resulted in a total of 11.6 percent of respondents to Wave 68 of the Pew Research Center’s American Trends Panel (ATP) reporting a residential move, either due to the pandemic or other causes. This migration rate was notably higher than the 9 percent annual migration rate reported by the U.S. Census Bureau for the same year[citation:txt1].

The initial wave of moves was primarily driven by health concerns and the sudden closures of college campuses. Young adults, in particular, were among the most affected demographic, with many relocating to their family homes or other safer locations to avoid exposure to the virus[citation:txt19]. As the pandemic progressed, the motivations for moving evolved, incorporating financial and educational factors alongside health-related concerns.

To better understand these changes, the Pew Research Center conducted two major surveys in June 2020 and November 2020. These surveys tracked the motivations and destinations of Americans who had relocated during the pandemic, offering a longitudinal perspective on how migration patterns shifted over time[citation:txt3]. The data for these surveys was drawn from the ATP, a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults recruited through a random sampling of residential addresses. The ATP is designed to ensure that nearly all U.S. adults have a chance of selection, and the survey is weighted to be representative of the U.S. adult population by gender, race, ethnicity, partisan affiliation, education, and other categories[citation:txt12].

The surveys also defined key demographic and income categories to facilitate nuanced analysis. For example, the definitions of "middle income," "lower income," and "upper income" were based on adjusted median family income, allowing for a more accurate comparison across different socioeconomic groups. These definitions were critical in understanding how various segments of the population experienced and responded to the pandemic differently[citation:txt1].

However, it is important to note some limitations of the survey, particularly in terms of representativeness. For instance, the Asian American sample included only English speakers, which may not fully represent the broader Asian American population. While the survey was conducted in multiple Asian languages and aimed to be representative of the six largest Asian subgroups in the U.S., the relatively small sample size and the focus on English speakers could limit the generalizability of the findings[citation:txt18].

By examining these early migration patterns and the methodology behind the Pew Research Center’s surveys, the foundation is laid for a deeper exploration of how and why Americans’ moving patterns evolved throughout the pandemic.
