# The Longest Peaceful Border and Its Maritime Disputes

The U.S.-Canada border is widely recognized as the longest peaceful and undefended land boundary in the world, stretching approximately 5,525 miles (8,891 km) across land and water. Despite its reputation for tranquility and cooperation, this vast boundary conceals a set of unresolved maritime and territorial disputes that could become flashpoints if political relations between the two nations ever deteriorate [citation:txt1]. While the two countries share a history of peaceful coexistence, the complexity of their shared geography and the ambiguity of historical treaties have left five key areas of contention in the maritime domain [citation:txt3].

These disputes, though often overlooked in the broader narrative of U.S.-Canada relations, are not merely academic or historical. They have real-world implications for resource management, environmental protection, and international law. The disputed areas include Machias Seal Island in the Gulf of Maine, the Beaufort Sea in the Arctic, and three other locations along the Pacific coast: the Strait of Juan de Fuca, the Dixon Entrance, and the Arctic waters near the Northwest Passage [citation:txt4]. Each of these areas reflects a unique combination of historical, legal, and geopolitical challenges that have yet to be resolved [citation:txt20].

The existence of these disputes is particularly intriguing given the otherwise cordial relationship between the United States and Canada. While the two nations have engaged in armed conflict in the past, such as during the War of 1812, the modern era has been marked by cooperation, mutual respect, and shared economic interests [citation:txt9]. However, the unresolved nature of these maritime boundaries suggests that even the most stable relationships can be complicated by historical grievances and legal ambiguities.

The importance of these disputes is underscored by recent developments, such as the 2024 announcement of a joint U.S.-Canadian task force to negotiate a resolution to the Beaufort Sea boundary. This initiative highlights the ongoing relevance of maritime boundary disputes and the potential for economic and strategic stakes, particularly in resource-rich Arctic waters [citation:txt10]. The resolution—or continued stalemate—of these disputes will likely shape the future of U.S.-Canada relations in ways that extend beyond the immediate issue of territorial demarcation.

As the article unfolds, it will explore the historical roots of these disputes, the legal frameworks that govern them, and the practical consequences they pose for both nations. The next section will delve into the specific history of the Machias Seal Island dispute, offering a case study in how historical treaties and events have contributed to the current state of maritime tensions.

# The Strait of Juan de Fuca Dispute: Equidistance, Base Points, and Provincial Divergence

The Strait of Juan de Fuca, a 154.5 km long waterway separating Vancouver Island in British Columbia from the Olympic Peninsula in Washington State, is a focal point of a maritime boundary dispute between the United States and Canada. While the international boundary runs clearly through the middle of the strait and is not in question, the seaward boundary extending 200 nautical miles (370 km) west from the strait’s mouth remains unresolved[citation:txt1]. The core of the dispute lies in the application of the principle of equidistance, which both countries agree should be used, but they differ in the selection of base points from which the equidistant line is drawn[citation:txt2]. These discrepancies have led to overlapping claims over a small but legally and ecologically significant area.

The principle of equidistance, a method of maritime boundary delimitation under international law, seeks to divide the waters between two states by drawing a line equidistant from their respective coasts. In the Strait of Juan de Fuca, both the U.S. and Canadian federal governments have proposed equidistant lines, but the disagreement arises from which coastal points are used as the starting points for these calculations. The result is two slightly different maritime boundaries, creating a small zone of overlap that remains contested[citation:txt14]. The area in dispute is approximately 15 square nautical miles, yet its resolution has been complicated by its potential implications for other unresolved maritime boundaries between the two countries[citation:txt18].

Adding another layer of complexity to the dispute is the stance of the Canadian province of British Columbia, which has rejected both federal proposals and instead advocates for a boundary based on the principle of natural prolongation. According to this principle, a state's maritime boundary should follow the natural geological extension of its landmass into the sea. British Columbia argues that the Juan de Fuca submarine canyon, a significant underwater feature, should serve as the boundary between the two countries[citation:txt16]. The province's position is rooted in the idea that the canyon represents a natural physiographic boundary, distinct from the artificial lines drawn by equidistance[citation:txt20].

This provincial claim, however, creates a dilemma for the Canadian federal government. If Canada were to adopt the natural prolongation argument for the Juan de Fuca Canyon, it could undermine its own position in the Gulf of Maine boundary dispute, where Canada favors an equidistant approach[citation:txt2]. The tension between federal and provincial interests highlights the broader challenge of balancing international legal consistency with regional economic and environmental concerns.

The Strait of Juan de Fuca dispute is emblematic of the broader unresolved maritime boundary issues between the U.S. and Canada. While the disagreement is relatively small in geographic scope, it underscores the complexities introduced by differing interpretations of international law and the influence of domestic political interests. As the next section will explore, similar tensions and historical legacies shape other maritime disputes along the Pacific and Atlantic coasts.

# The Dixon Entrance Dispute: A-B Line, Salmon Wars, and Gray Zones

The Dixon Entrance, a narrow strait separating the U.S. state of Alaska from the Canadian province of British Columbia, remains one of the most contentious and complex maritime boundary disputes between the two nations. Rooted in historical treaties and unresolved interpretations of boundary lines, the dispute has created a legal and practical gray zone that continues to impact fishing rights, enforcement, and international relations.

The origins of the conflict trace back to the 1825 Treaty of St. Petersburg, which established the initial boundary in the region that would later become the Alaska Panhandle. However, the area was largely unsurveyed at the time, and the treaty left room for interpretation. By the late 19th century, with the U.S. in control of Alaska and British Columbia a Canadian province, the discovery of gold in the region rekindled the dispute, bringing the issue to the forefront of diplomatic discussions [citation:txt2].

In 1903, a Court of Arbitration was established to resolve the boundary, resulting in the creation of the A-B Line. This line, drawn at approximately 54°40'N, was intended to delineate the boundary between the land masses of the two countries. Canada interprets the A-B Line as the international maritime boundary, asserting that nearly all of the Dixon Entrance lies within its internal waters. In contrast, the United States maintains that the A-B Line was only meant to define land sovereignty, and that the maritime boundary should be determined by international law—specifically, the equidistant principle. As a result, the U.S. unilaterally established its own maritime boundary in 1977, drawing a line south of the A-B Line [citation:txt4].

This divergence in interpretation has created four distinct zones in the Dixon Entrance. Two of these zones, located north of the A-B Line, are unclaimed by either country, while the two to the south are mutually claimed by both the U.S. and Canada. The overlapping claims have led to a “negative sovereignty” zone, where neither side exercises full jurisdiction, and enforcement is ambiguous [citation:txt10].

The practical consequences of this dispute are most evident in the fishing industry. The waters of the Dixon Entrance are rich in salmon, a vital resource for both the U.S. and Canadian fishing sectors. Because the U.S. does not recognize the A-B Line for purposes of fishing rights, it allows its fishermen to operate in areas that Canada considers its exclusive economic zone. This has led to direct conflict, including arrests, blockades, and the so-called “salmon wars,” where enforcement agencies from both countries have clashed over jurisdiction and compliance [citation:txt7].

The U.S. position is further complicated by its use of Nunez Rocks, a low-tide elevation located south of the A-B Line, as a basepoint for its territorial sea claims. While the U.S. does not recognize the Law of the Sea Treaty, it adheres to many of its principles as customary international law. This allows it to assert claims that go beyond the treaty’s provisions, particularly in areas where the tide recedes, leaving Nunez Rocks above water for part of the day [citation:txt10].

Despite the lack of a formal resolution, both countries continue to navigate the situation through diplomatic channels and cooperative agreements. However, the unresolved nature of the Dixon Entrance dispute remains a potential flashpoint, especially in times of heightened tension or resource competition. The historical and legal ambiguities surrounding the A-B Line underscore the broader challenges of defining maritime boundaries in international law and the ongoing need for clarity in border agreements [citation:txt9].

# The Beaufort Sea Dispute: Meridian vs. Perpendicular and Resource Stakes

The Beaufort Sea dispute represents one of the most significant unresolved maritime boundary issues between the United States and Canada. At the heart of the conflict is a wedge-shaped area north of the Alaska-Yukon border, where the two nations diverge sharply in their interpretations of how the land border should extend into the sea. Canada asserts that the 141st meridian, which forms the land boundary between Yukon and Alaska, should also serve as the maritime boundary, extending northward into the Arctic Ocean. In contrast, the United States advocates for a perpendicular line, which would create a more equidistant boundary and result in a triangular wedge of disputed territory approximately 6,250 square nautical miles in size [citation:txt2].

This disagreement originates from the 1825 Anglo-Russian Boundary Treaty, which defined the eastern border of Alaska as the “meridian line of the 141st degree, in its prolongation as far as the frozen ocean.” Canada interprets this language as establishing both a land and a maritime boundary along the 141st meridian. The U.S., however, argues that the treaty applies only to the land border and that the principle of equidistance should govern the maritime extension [citation:txt5]. The U.S. position is reinforced by its own historical application of equidistance in other Arctic boundary negotiations, such as with Russia in the Bering Strait [citation:txt10].

The dispute has long been influenced by the potential for oil and gas exploration in the region. Canada has issued oil and gas exploration permits and leases in the Beaufort Sea up to the 141st meridian, a move that the U.S. protested in 1976. Despite attempts to resolve the issue in the late 1970s, the U.S. and Canada were unable to reach a consensus and set the matter aside until the 2000s, when rising hydrocarbon prices reignited interest in the region. While the initial enthusiasm for a “race for Arctic resources” has since tempered, the presence of estimated 1.5 billion barrels of oil and significant natural gas reserves continues to make the Beaufort Sea a high-stakes geopolitical issue [citation:txt19].

The complexity of the dispute is further compounded by the principle of “equitable result,” a concept emphasized in international maritime law. Both nations have considered how an equitable boundary might balance geographic, legal, and historical factors. Canada has also invoked the historical usage of the area by Indigenous groups, such as the Inuvialuit, as a basis for its claims [citation:txt6]. However, the U.S. remains steadfast in its equidistance argument, maintaining that this method is the most legally and geographically appropriate solution [citation:txt8].

In 2024, both countries took a step toward resolution by establishing a joint task force to negotiate the Beaufort Sea boundary. This development, while promising, underscores the deep-rooted legal and political challenges in reconciling the two nations’ fundamentally different interpretations of the 1825 treaty [citation:txt9]. As climate change accelerates the melting of Arctic ice and opens new opportunities for resource extraction, the urgency of resolving the Beaufort Sea dispute is likely to grow [citation:txt1].

# The Northwest Passage Dispute: Sovereignty, Climate Change, and Legal Conflict

The Northwest Passage, a historically elusive sea route through the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, has become a focal point of unresolved sovereignty and navigational rights between the United States and Canada. The dispute centers on whether the passage constitutes Canadian internal waters, over which Canada exercises full control, or an international strait, where foreign vessels, including those of the United States, have the right to transit freely. Canada asserts its claim to the waters as internal territory, grounded in the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) and the precedent of archipelagic baselines, while the United States and other maritime nations maintain that the passage is an international waterway. The disagreement has deep historical roots, practical implications for Arctic navigation, and is increasingly influenced by the effects of climate change.

## Historical Exploration and the 1969 Incident

The Northwest Passage has long been a subject of exploration and strategic interest. For centuries, it was considered a mythical route connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. The 1969 voyage of the SS Manhattan, an icebreaking supertanker that successfully navigated the passage from the Beaufort Sea to the Davis Strait, marked a turning point in the dispute. Canada perceived this as a challenge to its sovereignty and responded by strengthening its legal and regulatory framework, including the extension of its territorial sea from 3 to 12 nautical miles and the enactment of the Arctic Waters Pollution Prevention Act. The U.S., meanwhile, viewed the voyage as a legitimate exercise of navigational rights under international law, setting the stage for a protracted legal and diplomatic conflict [citation:txt3].

## Legal Framework and the 1988 Arctic Cooperation Agreement

The legal disagreement is rooted in differing interpretations of UNCLOS. Canada argues that the passage qualifies as internal waters under Article 16 of the convention, as it is enclosed by a series of islands forming an archipelago. The U.S., however, contends that the passage meets the criteria of a strait used for international navigation under Article 37 of UNCLOS, which would grant it the right of transit passage. The dispute intensified in 1985 when the Canadian Coast Guard attempted to inspect an American vessel, an act that the U.S. government viewed as an infringement on its sovereign rights. In response to such incidents, the two nations signed the 1988 Arctic Cooperation Agreement, which established a framework for managing navigation through the passage. The agreement stipulated that the U.S. would notify Canada of its intentions to navigate the passage, and Canada would facilitate such navigation. While this arrangement has reduced overt conflict, the underlying legal dispute remains unresolved [citation:txt12].

## Climate Change and Increased Navigability

The most pressing factor influencing the Northwest Passage dispute is climate change. As global temperatures rise, sea ice in the Arctic is retreating at an unprecedented rate, making the passage more navigable for commercial and military vessels. Studies indicate that under 2°C of global warming, the passage could be navigable for up to 100 days a year, and with 4°C of warming, the navigable season could extend to 200–300 days annually. This increased accessibility raises the stakes for both nations, as the strategic and economic value of the passage grows. For Canada, the potential for increased traffic through its claimed internal waters heightens concerns over environmental protection and national sovereignty. For the U.S., the development of Arctic shipping routes is crucial to its economic and strategic interests, particularly in relation to Alaska [citation:txt10].

The shifting ice conditions also complicate enforcement and governance. As the passage becomes more accessible, the likelihood of foreign vessels attempting to transit without Canadian approval increases, potentially reigniting tensions. Moreover, the lack of a ratified international agreement on the passage’s status means that legal disputes may escalate as commercial and military activity in the region intensifies [citation:txt13].

# The Machias Seal Island Dispute: A Dry Land Claim in the Grey Zone

Machias Seal Island, a small, rocky outcrop less than 10 miles off the coast of Maine, remains the only dry land territory contested between the United States and Canada. Despite its diminutive size—just 550 meters long and 250 meters wide—the island lies at the heart of a longstanding dispute rooted in ambiguous historical treaties and evolving interpretations of maritime sovereignty [citation:txt20]. The island is located in a 277-square-mile stretch of ocean known as the “grey zone,” an area claimed by both nations and rich in marine resources, particularly lobster [citation:txt7].

The origins of the dispute trace back to the Treaty of Paris in 1783, which ended the American Revolutionary War and established the initial framework for U.S.-Canadian borders. However, the treaty left the maritime boundaries in the Gulf of Maine undefined, creating a legal gray area that neither country sought to resolve definitively in subsequent agreements such as the Jay Treaty (1794) or the Webster–Ashburton Treaty (1842) [citation:txt1]. The ambiguity persisted through the 19th and 20th centuries, and while most Gulf of Maine disputes were settled by the International Court of Justice in 1984, Machias Seal Island was notably excluded from that resolution [citation:txt4].

The island has been continuously occupied by Canadian authorities since 1832, when a lighthouse was constructed by the British to aid navigation in the busy shipping lanes of the Gulf of Maine. After Canadian Confederation in 1867, the lighthouse and the island were inherited by Canada, and it has remained under Canadian control ever since. Although the lighthouse is now automated, the Canadian Coast Guard continues to station lighthouse keepers on the island—ostensibly for operational purposes, but also as a symbolic assertion of Canadian sovereignty [citation:txt17].

While the island itself is of limited strategic or economic value, the surrounding waters are among the most productive fishing grounds in the region. Lobster fishing, in particular, has become a flashpoint in the dispute. In 2002, Canada unilaterally extended its jurisdiction into the grey zone, allowing Canadian fishermen to operate in the area during the summer months. This move brought them into direct conflict with U.S. fishermen from Maine, who view the waters as their own. Tensions have escalated into physical altercations, with reports of lobstermen cutting each other’s gear, making death threats, and even an incident in which a Maine fisherman lost a thumb while trying to free his traps from Canadian lines [citation:txt7].

The dispute has also seen recent flare-ups involving U.S. border patrol operations. In 2018, Canadian fishermen reported being harassed by U.S. enforcement vessels in the grey zone, reigniting diplomatic tensions between the two nations [citation:txt8]. While both countries have largely avoided formalizing the dispute through international courts, the ongoing presence of Canadian lighthouse keepers and the sporadic clashes between fishermen underscore the unresolved nature of the issue [citation:txt13].

The Machias Seal Island dispute, though relatively low-profile compared to other U.S.-Canada maritime conflicts, remains a symbol of the complexities of historical boundary-making and the enduring importance of resource-rich waters in international relations. As the climate continues to shift and marine ecosystems change, the stakes of this dispute are likely to remain high, with both nations watching the grey zone closely for any sign of escalation [citation:txt20].
