# The Global Phenomenon of Women Living Longer Than Men

Across the globe, one of the most consistent demographic patterns is that women tend to live longer than men. In 2021, this difference amounted to a significant 5-year gap in global life expectancy: the average life expectancy was 73.8 years for women versus 68.4 years for men [citation:txt1]. This phenomenon is not a recent development; it is observed in nearly all countries and across all racial groups, with women’s life expectancy consistently exceeding that of men of the same race by about five years, and with even larger disparities observed in some populations, such as Black women and Black men [citation:txt2]. The question of why this gap exists, and how it varies across age groups and regions, is central to understanding broader patterns of health and longevity.

The sex gap in life expectancy is not only a matter of longevity but also of mortality patterns throughout the human lifespan. Women tend to have lower mortality rates at nearly every age, with notable exceptions such as maternal mortality and, in certain regions, higher female childhood mortality [citation:txt3]. These variations suggest that the causes of the life expectancy gap are complex and multifaceted, involving a mix of biological, behavioral, and social factors.

One of the most prominent explanations for the female survival advantage is rooted in biological differences. Research suggests that evolutionary pressures may have favored female longevity to enhance offspring survival, resulting in a delayed onset of fatal conditions among women [citation:txt5]. Additionally, women tend to experience higher rates of non-fatal diseases, yet this does not translate into a higher overall mortality rate [citation:txt8]. However, while biological factors may provide a foundational explanation, the significant variation in the longevity gap across countries points toward the crucial role of social and behavioral determinants [citation:txt5].

For example, men are more likely to engage in riskier behaviors such as higher consumption of alcohol, tobacco, and fats, as well as being more prone to accidents and injuries [citation:txt5]. These behaviors contribute to higher male mortality from causes such as lung cancer, liver cirrhosis, and unintentional injuries [citation:txt8]. These gendered patterns of behavior and risk-taking are often influenced by societal norms and expectations around masculinity [citation:txt13], highlighting the interplay between biology and social context in shaping the life expectancy gap.

The article will explore whether this gap is primarily due to differences in mortality at older ages or if it is driven by higher risks among young men. It will also examine how the sex gap in life expectancy has changed over time and what factors have contributed to these shifts. By investigating these questions, the article aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the origins, variability, and historical evolution of the sex gap in life expectancy.
