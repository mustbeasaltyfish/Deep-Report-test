# The Global Phenomenon of Women Living Longer Than Men

Across the globe, one of the most consistent demographic patterns is that women tend to live longer than men. In 2021, this difference amounted to a significant 5-year gap in global life expectancy: the average life expectancy was 73.8 years for women versus 68.4 years for men [citation:txt1]. This phenomenon is not a recent development; it is observed in nearly all countries and has persisted for decades, with only a few exceptions in specific age groups or historical periods [citation:txt3].

The sex gap in life expectancy is not uniform across all age groups. While it is most pronounced in older ages, it also reflects disparities in mortality rates at younger ages. For instance, men tend to have higher mortality rates from chronic health conditions later in life, partly due to higher rates of smoking, alcohol, and drug use [citation:txt6]. However, the gap also emerges at younger ages, where men are more likely to die from injuries, whether unintentional (such as occupational accidents or car wrecks) or intentional (such as suicide) [citation:txt8]. This raises the critical question: is the overall sex gap in life expectancy primarily due to differences at older ages or to higher mortality risks for young men?

The origins of this gender-based survival advantage remain a subject of scientific debate. Some research suggests that biological factors, such as differences in genetic structure and hormonal profiles, may contribute to the longevity of women [citation:txt12]. Others argue that non-biological factors—such as lifestyle choices, risk-taking behaviors, and social roles—play a more significant role [citation:txt13]. For example, men have historically consumed more tobacco, alcohol, and fats than women in most societies and are more likely to die from diseases associated with these habits, such as lung cancer, tuberculosis, and cirrhosis [citation:txt8]. These behavioral differences often lead to higher male mortality from leading causes of death, including respiratory cancers, motor vehicle accidents, and liver disease [citation:txt9].

The sex gap in life expectancy is also influenced by historical and cultural contexts. In some countries, the gap has widened during periods of war or social upheaval, as seen in the United Kingdom during the World Wars [citation:txt10]. Conversely, the gap has narrowed in recent decades in some developed nations, such as the UK and the United States, due to improvements in male health behaviors and reductions in smoking rates [citation:txt18]. Yet, despite these improvements, women continue to outlive men, and the gap remains a persistent feature of global demographics.

This article will explore the origins, variability, and historical changes in the sex gap in life expectancy. It will examine whether the gap is primarily driven by mortality differences at older ages or by higher risks for young men, and it will investigate the factors that contribute to this disparity across different populations and time periods.

# Variability of the Sex Gap in Life Expectancy Across Countries and Time

While the global pattern of women outliving men is consistent, the size of the life expectancy gap varies significantly across countries and has evolved over time. In 2021, for example, the gender gap in life expectancy at birth averaged 5.4 years across OECD countries, with notable differences between regions. Central and Eastern European countries like Latvia, Lithuania, and Poland experienced the largest gaps, with women living eight or more years longer than men, while countries such as Iceland and Norway had much narrower gaps, at three years or less [citation:txt1]. These disparities reflect a complex interplay of historical, social, and health-related factors.

Historical events have played a crucial role in shaping the sex gap in life expectancy. Major conflicts such as wars have led to temporary but significant increases in male mortality, thereby widening the gap. For instance, during the 20th century, the life expectancy gap in many countries widened due to the high toll of wars on male populations. In Russia, the gap in life expectancy between women and men reached as high as 12 to 14 years in the 1990s, a period marked by economic and social upheaval following the dissolution of the Soviet Union [citation:txt6]. Similarly, the United States saw a peak in the sex gap at the end of the 1970s, while Sweden reached its peak in 1980 [citation:txt20]. These spikes underscore how external shocks can have long-lasting effects on demographic trends.

The 20th century also witnessed a general widening of the sex gap in life expectancy, particularly in industrialized nations. From the 1920s to the 1970s, the gap increased steadily in most developed countries due to rising male mortality from causes such as tobacco and alcohol use, occupational hazards, and higher rates of violent deaths [citation:txt17]. However, this trend began to reverse in the 1980s, especially in Western Europe, where the gap started to narrow. The decline in the sex gap has been attributed to improvements in public health, reductions in risky male behaviors, and greater access to healthcare for men. For example, in Japan, the gap peaked in the early 2000s but has since decreased, reflecting broader societal changes in health and lifestyle [citation:txt8].

More recently, the gap has continued to shrink in many countries, although the pace and extent of this narrowing differ. From 2002 to 2016, the sex gap in life expectancy in Western European countries decreased by an average of 18.0%, the most significant reduction observed among the regions studied [citation:txt4]. The United Kingdom, for instance, saw the gap drop from 4.5 years to 3.5 years over this period. In contrast, countries in Eastern Europe, such as Russia, experienced smaller reductions, with the gap decreasing from 11.2 years in 2014 to 8.7 years on average by 2021 [citation:txt5]. The United States, while showing some decline, still maintains a larger gap than most comparable countries, with a 5.3-year difference between women and men as of 2023 [citation:txt11].

These variations can also be visualized in global data. The map of the 2023 sex gap in life expectancy highlights countries with the largest and smallest disparities [citation:img7]. Russia, Belarus, and Ukraine stand out for their large gaps, with female life expectancy exceeding male life expectancy by more than 10 years. In contrast, countries in Asia and parts of Africa exhibit much smaller gaps, with some, like Nepal and Pakistan, showing differences of less than half a year [citation:txt12]. Such data illustrate how the sex gap is not a fixed phenomenon but one that is shaped by local conditions, including healthcare infrastructure, cultural norms, and economic development.

The historical trajectory of the sex gap in life expectancy thus reveals a dynamic interplay between biological, behavioral, and environmental factors. While the gap has generally narrowed in recent decades, it remains a significant public health and social issue, particularly in regions where male mortality is disproportionately high. Understanding these patterns is essential for developing targeted interventions aimed at reducing gender disparities in health outcomes and promoting longevity for all.

# Understanding Period Life Expectancy and Its Influences

Period life expectancy is a statistical measure that summarizes the average number of years a person is expected to live based on the current age-specific death rates in a given year. It does not predict the actual lifespan of individuals but rather provides a snapshot of mortality conditions at a specific point in time. This metric is calculated by applying current death rates across all age groups to a hypothetical cohort of individuals born in that year, estimating how long they would live under those same conditions throughout their lives [citation:txt2].

Period life expectancy is highly sensitive to changes in health, behavior, and major historical events. For example, during wars or pandemics, life expectancy can drop significantly due to the increased mortality rates. The global HIV/AIDS epidemic in the late 20th century and the recent impact of the COVID-19 pandemic have both had measurable effects on life expectancy, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and other regions with vulnerable health systems [citation:txt7]. Similarly, improvements in healthcare, sanitation, and public health policies have historically led to increases in life expectancy, as seen in the post-World War II period in many developed countries [citation:txt20].

It is important to note that period life expectancy reflects average differences between populations and does not imply that all individuals within a group will experience the same outcomes. While women, on average, live longer than men globally, there are still many men who outlive women in their respective populations. This variation is influenced by a complex interplay of biological, behavioral, and sociocultural factors that affect individual health outcomes [citation:txt11]. For instance, some men may benefit from healthier lifestyles or better access to healthcare, while others may face unique risks that shorten their life expectancy [citation:txt13].

The data on life expectancy is also shaped by broader societal trends such as economic development, access to education, and gender roles. In wealthier nations, where healthcare systems are robust and preventive care is widely available, the gap between male and female life expectancy tends to be more pronounced. In contrast, in lower-income countries, especially those with high maternal and childhood mortality rates, the gap may be smaller or even reversed in some cases [citation:txt6]. As countries develop and adopt modern medical practices, the global trend has been toward a more consistent female advantage in life expectancy [citation:txt1].

# The Age-Specific Sources of the Sex Gap in Life Expectancy

The sex gap in life expectancy is not a uniform phenomenon; it emerges and persists at different stages of the human lifespan. Males consistently exhibit higher death rates than females across infancy, youth, and older age, each contributing uniquely to the overall disparity in life expectancy. Understanding these age-specific patterns is essential for identifying the biological, behavioral, and social mechanisms that underpin the sex gap in mortality.

## Infant Mortality: Biological Vulnerabilities

Infant boys are biologically more vulnerable than girls, resulting in higher mortality rates during the first year of life. This disparity is evident globally and is rooted in a combination of genetic predispositions, higher susceptibility to infections, and complications related to prematurity. Before 1970, the primary causes of male infant mortality were infections and chronic degenerative diseases, but since then, improvements in medical care have disproportionately benefited male infants, narrowing the gap in some regions while highlighting persistent disparities in others [citation:txt12]. For example, in 2023, the infant mortality rates for boys exceeded those for girls in many African and Asian countries, with the Central African Republic and Niger showing some of the highest rates [![](citation:img1)].

## Youth Mortality: External Causes and Risk Behaviors

The sex gap in life expectancy widens significantly during youth, particularly between the ages of 15 and 49. During this stage, young men face a disproportionately higher risk of death from external causes such as accidents, violence, and suicide. In 2019, men and boys accounted for 80% of deaths due to homicide, 75% for road traffic injuries, and 69% for suicide [citation:txt2]. These trends are consistent across multiple regions, with the U.S., Russia, and Japan showing particularly stark differences in mortality rates between young men and women [citation:txt19]. Road traffic injuries remain the leading cause of death among boys and young men aged 15–29 years, while homicide ranks as the second leading cause in this age group [citation:txt2]. Additionally, global suicide mortality rates were 75% higher in men than in women in 2016, with transport-related deaths also showing a significant gender gap [![](citation:img10)].

## Older Age Mortality: Chronic Diseases and Risk Behaviors

In older age, the sex gap in life expectancy is sustained due to higher death rates in men from chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease, cancer, and respiratory illnesses. These disparities are partly explained by long-standing differences in health behaviors, including higher rates of smoking, alcohol consumption, and sedentary lifestyles among men. For example, studies have shown that men are more likely to engage in risky behaviors such as heavy drinking and cigarette smoking, which contribute to elevated risks of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), liver cirrhosis, and coronary heart disease [citation:txt5]. In addition, men tend to delay seeking medical care and are less likely to adhere to preventive health measures, further exacerbating their mortality risk [citation:txt11]. 

The impact of these behaviors is reflected in mortality trends. For instance, in the U.S., age-standardized mortality rates for cardiovascular disease remain significantly higher among older men than women, with similar patterns observed in other high-income countries [![](citation:img5)]. Moreover, the decline in smoking rates among men over the past few decades has not kept pace with the reduction in rates among women, contributing to a persistent gap in life expectancy [citation:txt5].

# Historical Shifts in the Contributions of Different Age Groups to the Sex Gap in Life Expectancy

The sex gap in life expectancy has not remained static over time, nor has it been uniformly driven by the same age groups in different historical periods. Historically, infant mortality played a significant role in determining the life expectancy gap between men and women. In the early 20th century, for example, in countries such as France and Denmark, excess male infant deaths accounted for more than half of the overall sex gap in life expectancy [citation:txt19]. This was partly due to the biological vulnerability of male infants and also to underreporting of female infant deaths in some regions [citation:txt19]. However, as public health interventions improved and infant and child mortality rates declined, the contribution of early life stages to the sex gap diminished significantly [citation:txt17].

Instead, the sex gap has increasingly been influenced by mortality differences in middle and older age groups. For instance, in the United Kingdom, the sex gap at birth peaked in the 1940s, but the gap at older ages, such as 65 and 80, also grew significantly during this time. By the 21st century, while the overall sex gap had somewhat narrowed, the largest contributions to the gap came from older adults, with the 80+ age group accounting for a small but notable portion [citation:img1]. This trend is not unique to the UK. In many Western European countries, the United States, and Japan, the largest contributors to the sex gap in life expectancy have shifted to older age groups, particularly those over 70 years of age [citation:txt6].

This shift can be attributed to several factors. One major contributor has been the rise and subsequent decline in smoking rates among men. In the 20th century, the widespread adoption of smoking among men, particularly in industrialized countries, significantly widened the sex gap in life expectancy [citation:txt3]. Smoking-related diseases such as cardiovascular disease and lung cancer disproportionately affected male populations, especially in middle and older age [citation:txt7]. However, as anti-smoking campaigns and public health interventions took effect, mortality rates from smoking-related diseases have declined, particularly among older men, helping to narrow the sex gap [citation:txt3]. In the United States, for example, lung cancer death rates for men peaked in the 1990s and have since declined, while rates for women have also decreased but at a slower pace [citation:img7].

The impact of major historical events, particularly wars, cannot be overlooked. The World Wars, for instance, caused a dramatic spike in the sex gap due to the disproportionately high mortality of young and middle-aged men in combat. In the aftermath of these conflicts, the sex gap in life expectancy temporarily widened as a result of the large number of male deaths [citation:txt1]. This was particularly evident in countries such as Russia and Poland, where the gap in life expectancy between women and men remained large for decades due to high male mortality in youth and middle age [citation:txt6]. In these countries, the 60–79 age group continues to account for the most significant contribution to the sex gap in life expectancy [citation:img6].

Furthermore, the patterns of mortality contributions to the sex gap vary across countries. In Eastern European nations, for example, the largest contributions to the sex gap are still observed at relatively younger ages compared to Western European countries and the United States [citation:txt6]. In Russia, for instance, external causes of death—such as accidents, violence, and alcohol-related deaths—among men aged 25–30 and 40–45 years continue to play a major role in the large sex gap [citation:txt6]. These country-specific variations highlight the influence of social, economic, and cultural factors on mortality patterns and the life expectancy gap [citation:txt11].

In summary, the historical trajectory of the sex gap in life expectancy has been shaped by a complex interplay of biological, social, and medical factors. While infant and early childhood mortality once dominated the gap, the contributions of middle and older age groups have become more significant over time. The role of smoking, war-related mortality, and country-specific patterns of male mortality have all played critical roles in shaping these shifts. As public health interventions continue to evolve, understanding these historical trends remains essential for addressing the ongoing disparities in life expectancy between men and women.

# Summary and Conclusion

The sex gap in life expectancy is a persistent and multifaceted phenomenon that is evident from birth and continues throughout the lifespan. This gap is not uniform across countries or over time, and it is shaped by a complex interplay of biological, behavioral, and social factors. From the moment of birth, boys face higher mortality risks than girls, partly due to biological vulnerabilities such as genetic disorders and higher susceptibility to infections [citation:txt20]. These early differences set the stage for a lifelong disparity in life expectancy, which is further influenced by external and behavioral factors during youth and adulthood.

Historically, the primary contributor to the sex gap was infant mortality, with significant differences in survival rates between boys and girls during the first year of life [citation:txt10]. However, as public health interventions have improved and infant and child mortality rates have declined, the main sources of the gap have shifted to mortality differences in older age groups [citation:txt6]. Today, the sex gap is largely driven by higher male mortality in middle and older adulthood, particularly due to chronic diseases such as heart disease, cancer, and lifestyle-related conditions like smoking and alcohol abuse [citation:txt7].

The size and sources of the sex gap in life expectancy vary significantly between countries. In some nations, such as Japan and Sweden, the gap is relatively small and stable, while in others, like Russia and China, it has been marked by dramatic fluctuations and larger disparities [citation:img2]. These differences are influenced by a range of factors, including national health policies, cultural norms, economic development, and gender-specific health behaviors [citation:txt17]. For example, in countries where smoking rates among men have historically been high, the impact on male mortality has played a central role in shaping the life expectancy gap [citation:txt18].

Over time, the sex gap has also shown a tendency to narrow, particularly in high-income countries. This narrowing is largely attributed to changing health behaviors, especially in relation to smoking and alcohol consumption. As more women have adopted behaviors that were previously more common among men, such as smoking, the delayed health consequences have started to close the gap [citation:txt2]. Additionally, improvements in healthcare access and treatment for chronic diseases have contributed to a reduction in the male mortality advantage at older ages [citation:txt17].

In conclusion, the sex gap in life expectancy is a dynamic and evolving phenomenon. While biological factors play a foundational role, the majority of the gap can be attributed to non-biological influences such as lifestyle choices, social roles, and environmental conditions. Understanding the historical shifts and current variations in the gap is essential for developing targeted public health policies that address the specific needs of men and women across different stages of life [citation:txt1]. As the global landscape of health continues to change, ongoing research and policy innovation will be critical in further reducing disparities and improving life expectancy for all.
