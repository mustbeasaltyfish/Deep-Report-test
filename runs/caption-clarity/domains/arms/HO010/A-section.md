# The Global Phenomenon of Women Living Longer Than Men

Across the globe, one of the most consistent demographic patterns is that women tend to live longer than men. In 2021, this difference amounted to a significant 5-year gap in global life expectancy: the average life expectancy was 73.8 years for women versus 68.4 years for men [citation:txt1]. This phenomenon is not a recent development; it is observed in nearly all countries and across diverse racial and ethnic groups [citation:txt2]. For example, within racial groups, women’s life expectancy typically exceeds that of men by about five years, with somewhat larger differences observed between Black women and Black men [citation:txt2].

The reasons for this gender gap in life expectancy are complex and multifaceted. While biological differences may play a role—such as hormonal and genetic factors that influence longevity—research suggests that a significant portion of the gap can be attributed to behavioral and social factors [citation:txt5]. Men, on average, tend to engage in riskier behaviors, including higher consumption of alcohol and tobacco, and are more likely to be involved in dangerous occupations or activities that lead to injury or premature death [citation:txt8]. These differences contribute to higher mortality rates among men, particularly at older ages, where chronic health conditions such as heart disease and cancer disproportionately affect them [citation:txt6].

It is also important to consider how the sex gap in life expectancy has evolved over time. Historically, the gap has fluctuated due to various factors, including wars, public health crises, and changes in lifestyle and health behaviors. For example, during the World Wars, the gap in life expectancy between men and women temporarily widened due to the disproportionately high mortality rates among men in conflict zones [citation:txt10]. More recently, however, the gap has shown a slight narrowing in some regions, potentially due to improvements in men’s health behaviors, such as reduced smoking and increased awareness of health risks [citation:txt18].

This article will explore the origins, variations, and historical shifts in the sex gap in life expectancy. It will investigate whether the gap is primarily driven by differences in mortality at older ages or by higher risks for young men. Additionally, it will examine how the gap varies across countries and how it has changed over time, shedding light on the broader social, economic, and biological factors that shape this persistent demographic phenomenon.
