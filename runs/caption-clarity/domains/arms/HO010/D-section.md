# The Global Phenomenon of Women Living Longer Than Men

Across the globe, one of the most consistent demographic patterns is that women tend to live longer than men. In 2021, this difference amounted to a significant 5-year gap in global life expectancy: the average life expectancy was 73.8 years for women versus 68.4 years for men [citation:txt1]. This phenomenon is not a recent development; it is observed in nearly all countries and has persisted for decades, with only a few exceptions in specific age groups or historical periods [citation:txt3].

The sex gap in life expectancy is not uniform across all age groups. While it is most pronounced in older ages, it also reflects disparities in mortality rates at younger ages. For instance, men tend to have higher mortality rates from chronic health conditions later in life, partly due to higher rates of smoking, alcohol, and drug use [citation:txt6]. However, the gap also emerges at younger ages, where men are more likely to die from injuries, whether unintentional (such as occupational accidents or car wrecks) or intentional (such as suicide) [citation:txt8]. This raises the critical question: is the overall sex gap in life expectancy primarily due to differences at older ages or to higher mortality risks for young men?

The origins of this gender-based survival advantage remain a subject of scientific debate. Some research suggests that biological factors, such as differences in genetic structure and hormonal profiles, may contribute to the longevity of women [citation:txt12]. Others argue that non-biological factors—such as lifestyle choices, risk-taking behaviors, and social roles—play a more significant role [citation:txt13]. For example, men have historically consumed more tobacco, alcohol, and fats than women in most societies and are more likely to die from diseases associated with these habits, such as lung cancer, tuberculosis, and cirrhosis [citation:txt8]. These behavioral differences often lead to higher male mortality from leading causes of death, including respiratory cancers, motor vehicle accidents, and liver disease [citation:txt9].

The sex gap in life expectancy is also influenced by historical and cultural contexts. In some countries, the gap has widened during periods of war or social upheaval, as seen in the United Kingdom during the World Wars [citation:txt10]. Conversely, the gap has narrowed in recent decades in some developed nations, such as the UK and the United States, due to improvements in male health behaviors and reductions in smoking rates [citation:txt18]. Yet, despite these improvements, women continue to outlive men, and the gap remains a persistent feature of global demographics.

This article will explore the origins, variability, and historical changes in the sex gap in life expectancy. It will examine whether the gap is primarily driven by mortality differences at older ages or by higher risks for young men, and it will investigate the factors that contribute to this disparity across different populations and time periods.
