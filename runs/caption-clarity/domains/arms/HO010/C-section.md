# The Global Phenomenon of Women Living Longer Than Men

Across the globe, one of the most consistent demographic patterns is that women tend to live longer than men. In 2021, this difference amounted to a significant 5-year gap in global life expectancy: the average life expectancy was 73.8 years for women versus 68.4 years for men [citation:txt1]. This phenomenon is not limited to a few countries or regions but is observed almost universally [citation:txt3]. Even within racial groups, women tend to outlive men by about five years, with some of the largest differences seen between Black women and Black men [citation:txt2].

The persistence of this gender gap in life expectancy raises a number of compelling questions. Why do women consistently live longer than men? What factors contribute to this disparity, and how do they vary across age groups and geographic regions? Has the gap always been this wide, or has it changed over time? These are the central questions this article will address, exploring the origins, variability, and historical shifts in the sex gap in life expectancy.

The sex gap in life expectancy is not solely a result of differences at older ages, where men typically experience higher mortality rates due to chronic health conditions such as heart disease and cancer [citation:txt6]. While these factors are significant, the gap is also influenced by higher risks for young men, particularly in terms of behavioral and environmental factors such as smoking, alcohol use, and risky behaviors [citation:txt5]. Moreover, some of the mortality differences can be traced back to childhood, with female mortality being higher than male in certain regions for ages 0 to 4 [citation:txt3]. These early-life disparities, along with skewed sex ratios at birth in some parts of the world, further complicate the picture [citation:txt3].

Understanding the sex gap in life expectancy requires a multifaceted approach, as both biological and social factors play roles in shaping this inequality. Some researchers argue that evolutionary advantages in female longevity have contributed to the survival of women over men, particularly in post-reproductive years [citation:txt13]. Others emphasize the impact of social and behavioral factors, such as men's higher rates of smoking, drinking, and exposure to occupational risks [citation:txt8]. As the following sections will explore in greater depth, the interplay of these factors varies across countries and historical periods, making the sex gap in life expectancy a complex and evolving phenomenon.
