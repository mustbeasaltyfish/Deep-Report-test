# Defining Blue-Collar Workers and the Survey Methodology

Blue-collar workers have long held a prominent place in public discourse, often symbolizing the backbone of the American workforce. These individuals engage in manual or physical labor and are typically found in sectors such as manufacturing, construction, transportation, and service industries. In recent years, their roles and perceptions of their jobs have become a focal point for researchers and policymakers, especially as automation and economic shifts reshape the labor market. To better understand how blue-collar workers in the U.S. perceive their jobs compared to other workers, the Pew Research Center conducted a comprehensive survey in October 2024.

For the purposes of this analysis, blue-collar workers are defined as those who perform manual or physical labor in one of five key industrial sectors: manufacturing, mining and construction; agriculture, forestry, fishing, and hunting; retail and trade; hospitality or service; and transportation. This classification ensures that the analysis focuses on individuals whose work is physically demanding and often involves direct interaction with tools, machinery, or materials. Together, these sectors account for approximately 27% of the U.S. workforce, or about 1,004 individuals in the survey sample [citation:txt14].

The survey was conducted among 5,273 employed U.S. adults, with 1,004 of them classified as blue-collar workers based on the criteria outlined above. The data collection period spanned from October 7 to October 13, 2024, and the sample was designed to be representative of the broader U.S. workforce. The methodology ensures that the findings can be generalized to the larger population of blue-collar workers, while also allowing for meaningful comparisons with other employed workers in white-collar or non-manual occupations [citation:txt4].

One of the key reasons for using a sectoral and labor-based classification is to maintain consistency with how these workers are categorized in economic and labor statistics. Historically, blue-collar work has been associated with physical labor and the use of tools or machinery, distinguishing it from white-collar work, which typically involves office-based, intellectual tasks [citation:txt13]. However, the modern labor market has blurred these lines in some cases, as skilled trades and technical roles within blue-collar sectors may require advanced training or education. Despite these nuances, the classification used in this survey remains grounded in the traditional understanding of blue-collar work, focusing on the physical nature of the labor and the industries in which it is most prevalent [citation:txt14].

This survey provides a foundation for exploring how blue-collar workers view their jobs in terms of satisfaction, career prospects, and opportunities for advancement.
